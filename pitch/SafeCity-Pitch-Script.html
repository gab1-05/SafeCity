Part 1 of 4

The Hook & The Problem

Speaker 1 · 0:00 – 1:15 · ~165 words · tone: calm, conversational, then serious

Goal: make them feel the problem before they hear the product Do not: mention features, tech, or the product name yet

[Step forward. Pause for one second before speaking. Make eye contact across the room.]

Imagine you report a pothole. You call the helpline. Someone says, “We’ve forwarded it.”

Then nothing.

[Beat. Let that land.]

That is not a technology problem. It is a system problem. In our cities today, civic complaints arrive through phone calls, WhatsApp messages, walk-in visits and emails — each going to a different place. And so nobody can say what is still pending, who is actually working on it, or whether “forwarded” means anything at all.

Five failures repeat in every city. One: there is no single place to report. Two: there is no shared language for status, so “in progress” and “ignored” look identical. Three: there is no record of who acted, or when. Four: there are no deadlines — so quiet problems like streetlights get starved, while whatever is complained about loudest jumps the queue. And five: there is no way for the citizen to check the outcome.

So people stop reporting. [Slower.] And when citizens stop reporting, the city loses the one signal it needs to function.

So we asked a simple question: what if every complaint had a home, a deadline, and a public trail?

Handoff: turn to Speaker 2 and say — “That is exactly what SafeCity does. (Name)” — then step back. Speaker 2 begins immediately; no gap, no overlap.
Part 2 of 4

The Solution & Features

Speaker 2 · 1:15 – 2:50 · ~200 words · tone: warm, confident, a little faster

Goal: walk them through one complaint, start to finish Key device: count the four steps on your fingers

That is SafeCity — a smart city incident management and civic response platform. One place where a citizen reports a civic problem, and the right department actually fixes it.

Here is the journey. A citizen opens the platform and reports in four steps. [Count them off.] What kind of problem. Where it is on the map. A photo as evidence. How severe it is. They get a reference number they can track — even without an account — and they can report anonymously if they prefer.

From there, the platform does the quiet, critical work. It routes the report to the department that owns that category: potholes to roads, leaks to water, sparking wires to electricity. It sets a deadline based on how serious the problem is, and it watches that deadline. If the deadline is at risk, it escalates automatically — so nothing depends on someone remembering.

Department staff then work from a single queue, sorted by severity and deadline — not by who complained loudest. They verify, assign, start work, and resolve. And resolution requires proof: a photograph of the finished fix. A closure you cannot inspect is just a ticket someone wanted off their list.

Finally, the citizen is notified, confirms the repair, and rates it. If the fix did not last, they can reopen it. [Beat.] The status is not final until they agree.

Underneath all of this, every single action is written to an unchangeable log — and a live public map shows the neighbourhood what is being done.

Handoff: “But a platform is only as useful as the people it serves &mdash and as honest as the boundaries it admits to. (Name)?”
Part 3 of 4

Scope & Stakeholders

Speaker 3 · 2:50 – 4:10 · ~160 words · tone: precise, steady, authoritative

Goal: show discipline — knowing what not to build Must say: the disclaimer — it is scripted below, word for word

Who is this for, and where does it end?

SafeCity serves six groups. Citizens report, track, and confirm fixes. Department staff work the queue. Emergency responders get a priority lane for high-severity incidents. Volunteers keep an eye on community activity. Department heads balance workload and handle escalations. And city administrators configure categories and deadlines, manage users, and review the full audit trail.

The scope matters as much as the features. In scope: registration, reporting with photo evidence, the complete workflow from verification to closure, deadline enforcement, notifications, dashboards, analytics and administration.

Out of scope — and we state this openly: [Slower — this is the trust moment.] SafeCity is not an official emergency dispatch service. In a real emergency, you call your local emergency number. It is not an officially integrated government system. There are no native mobile apps yet, and no payments. Every external connection sits behind a switch we can turn on later, with approval.

Saying clearly what a product will not do is not a weakness. It is the difference between a project and a promise.

Handoff: “Which raises the real question — why is this any better than what already exists? (Name)?”
Part 4 of 4

Impact, Difference & Future

Speaker 4 · 4:10 – 5:45 · ~185 words · tone: assured, building to a clean finish

Goal: leave them with three differences and one sentence each After: deliver the 4-line recap on page 7, then thank them

So what makes this different from a complaint form?

Three things.

First — the workflow is enforced, not optional. A status cannot skip its steps, and “fixed” can never be entered without a record of who entered it and when. Nobody is exempt from that trail — not even the superuser.

Second — privacy is built in, not bolted on. The public never sees who reported an incident or exactly where they live; they see the neighbourhood. The authorities see the truth. And a citizen who fears reprisal can report anonymously.

Third — the AI only suggests. It can recommend a category, flag severity, or spot a duplicate report. But a human always makes the decision, and the system records which one. No machine ever acts alone.

[Beat.] The impact is measurable: faster response, visible accountability, and a feedback loop that earns back citizen trust — because reporting now visibly leads to results.

Where next? Native mobile apps, regional language support, smarter analytics, and integration with city systems the day approvals exist.

[Turn fully to the evaluators. Slow down. This is the close.] SafeCity, in four sentences: A citizen reports a problem. Four roles act on it. Nothing can be done silently. And nothing is finished until the citizen agrees.

Thank you — we’re happy to take your questions.

Finish: all four step forward together, stand still, and wait. Do not shuffle, do not whisper, do not start packing up.
Cheat Sheet

The four-sentence recap

Speaker 4 delivers these after their section, not during it. Rehearse until automatic.

1
A citizen reports a problem. Four steps: what, where, photo, severity — and a reference number they can track.
2
Four roles act on it. Staff verify and fix, responders handle emergencies, heads balance load, administrators audit everything.
3
Nothing can be done silently. Every action writes an unchangeable record — deadlines escalate on their own.
4
Nothing is finished until the citizen agrees. They confirm the fix, rate it, or reopen it.

If asked “what is this in one line?”

“SafeCity turns scattered civic complaints into one tracked, time-bound, publicly visible workflow — from the citizen’s report to the citizen’s confirmation.”

If asked “what did you deliberately leave out?”

“Emergency dispatch, government system integration, native apps and payments. We scoped them out honestly rather than claiming them — external integrations are designed behind interfaces we can enable later, with approval.”
